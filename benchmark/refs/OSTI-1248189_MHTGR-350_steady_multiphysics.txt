Abstract 
The PHISICS code system has been under development at INL since 2010. It consists 
of several modules providing improved coupled core simulation capability: INSTANT 
(3D nodal transport core calculations), MRTAU (depletion and decay heat generation) 
and modules performing criticality searches, fuel shuffling and generalized perturbation. 
Coupling of the PHISICS code suite to the thermal hydraulics system code RELAP5-3D 
was finalized in 2013, and as part of the verification and validation effort the first phase 
of the OECD/NEA MHTGR-350 Benchmark has now been completed.  
The theoretical basis and latest development status of the coupled PHISICS/RELAP5-
3D tool are described in more detail in a concurrent paper. This paper provides an 
overview of the OECD/NEA MHTGR-350 Benchmark and presents the results of 
Exercises 2 and 3 defined for Phase I. Exercise 2 required the modelling of a stand-
alone thermal fluids solution at End of Equilibrium Cycle for the Modular High 
Temperature Reactor (MHTGR). The RELAP5-3D results of four sub-cases are 
discussed, consisting of various combinations of coolant bypass flows and material 
thermophysical properties. Exercise 3 required a coupled neutronics and thermal fluids 
solution, and the PHISICS/RELAP5-3D code suite was used to calculate the results of 
two sub-cases.   
The main focus of the paper is a comparison of results obtained with the traditional 
RELAP5-3D “ring” model approach against a much more detailed model that include 
kinetics feedback on individual block level and thermal feedbacks on a triangular sub-
mesh. The higher fidelity that can be obtained by this “block” model is illustrated with 
comparison results on the temperature, power density and flux distributions. It is shown 
===PAGE 5===
4 
 
that the ring model leads to significantly lower  fuel temperatures (up to 10%) when 
compared with the higher fidelity block model, and that the additional model 
development and run-time efforts are worth the gains obtained in the improved spatial 
temperature and flux distributions. 
Keywords: HTGR, RELAP5-3D, PHISICS 
I. INTRODUCTION 
 
The High Temperature Gas-Cooled Reactor (HTGR) Methods Core Simulation group 
at the Idaho National Laboratory (INL) leads the development of the Organization for 
Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) MHTGR-350 Transient Benchmark 
for evaluating prismatic HTGR analysis codes. The benchmark is sponsored by the 
OECD's Nuclear Energy Agency, and will eventually yield a set of peer-reviewed steady-
state, transient, and lattice solutions that can be used by the Department of Energy, 
Nuclear Regulatory Commission and vendors to assess their codes. The technical 
specification is based on the General Atomics 350 MW Modular High Temperature Gas-
Cooled Reactor (MHTGR) design 
1,2. The HTGR Methods group at INL is responsible 
for defining the benchmark specifications, lead the data collection and comparison 
activities, and chair the annual technical workshops. Several code development and 
verification activities from participants in Germany, Korea and the United States are 
currently based on the MHTGR-350 benchmark, and progress on the INL contributions 
have already been completed 
3,4,5.  
As part of the benchmark simulation activities, the Parallel and Highly Innovative 
Simulation for INL Code System (PHISICS) 6 reactor physics package was coupled to 
the INL-developed system thermal analyses code RELAP5-3D 7 to enable neutron 
transport calculations for the core as well as more flexible cross section handling in 
===PAGE 6===
5 
 
coupled core simulations. RELAP5-3D typically does not provide the spatial resolution 
of higher fidelity thermal fluid codes but it does have a well-validated plant simulation 
capability.  
This paper summarizes the INL results obtained for Exercises 2 and 3 defined for 
Phase I of the benchmark, with a specific focus on a comparison of the traditional 
homogenized (ring) model approach with a much more detailed triangular geometry 
model for the thermal-hydraulic solution with RELAP5-3D. These two models were 
developed during the coupling of the PHISICS/RELAP5-3D system to investigate the 
effect of spatial refinements in the temperature fields on the typical parameters of 
interest (fuel temperatures, k
eff), and to assess the suitability of utilizing a systems code 
like RELAP5-3D to provide detailed temperature profiles.  
 
II. OECD/NEA MHTGR-350 BENCHMARK OVERVIEW 
 
The MHTGR-350 MW benchmark specification was developed in cooperation with 
General Atomics to compare different codes and methods, since validation data from 
current or past prismatic HTGR experimental facilities or reactors are severely limited. 
The scope of the benchmark is to establish a well-defined problem based on a common 
given data set, and to compare methods and tools in core simulation and thermal 
hydraulic analysis through a set of multi-dimensional computational test problems. The 
MHTGR-350 MW benchmark is based on the approach followed for the OECD/NEA 
PBMR-400 MW benchmark 
8. It consists of three Phase I steady-state exercises, four 
Phase II transient exercises, and two Phase III depletion exercises. The focus of this 
RELAP5-3D comparison paper is on the results obtained for the Exercises 2 and 3 of 
===PAGE 7===
6 
 
Phase I, as summarized below. (The stand-alone neutronics results obtained for 
Exercise 1 were included in a 2013 INL status report 4, and will not be discussed here).  
• Exercise 1: Neutronics only steady-state solution for the 350 MW End of Equilibrium 
Cycle (EOEC) MHTGR core, using the specified geometry, material descriptions, 
and detailed cross-section libraries. 
• Exercise 2: Thermal hydraulics only steady-state core solution. Four sub-cases are 
defined, depending on the core bypass flow type and the use of fixed or variable 
thermophysical material properties. Participants are expected to provide steady-
state solutions for each of these sub-cases according to their codes' capabilities. A 
defined core power distribution map must be used, since no neutronics are involved 
in this exercise. 
• Exercise 3: Coupled neutronics-thermal hydraulic core steady state. This exercise is 
a combination of the first two exercises and the coupled steady-state solution must 
be calculated using the provided temperature dependent cross-section library and 
burnup distributions. 
The axial and radial core layouts of the MHTGR-350 design are shown in Fig. 1 and 
Fig. 2, respectively, and the major core characteristics are summarized in Table I. (In 
Fig. 1, the Control Rods (CR), Core Barrel (CB) and Reactor Pressure Vessel (RPV) are 
also indicated). A detailed description of the MHTGR-350 design can be found in the 
benchmark specification document (currently still in draft format 
2). The 1/3rd core 
numbering utilized for the benchmark data reporting, and the discussion that follows in 
this paper, is shown in Fig. 3 for the first (bottom) layer of the core and reflector regions.

III. CODE AND MODEL DESCRIPTION  
This section provides an overview of the simulation codes utilized for this 
benchmark, as well as a discussion of the models developed and major assumptions 
that were made.  
III.A. The Phisics Code Suite 
The complex phenomena in Generation IV systems such as the MHTGR design 
challenge legacy system codes like RELAP5-3D, which were developed for best 
estimate transient simulation of light water reactors 7. RELAP5-3D is able to model 
coupled behaviour of the plant system (heat exchangers, steam generators, pumps, 
valves, etc.), thermal-hydraulics of the reactor core and the neutronics of the reactor. 
The code was specifically designed for simulations of light water reactor transients such 
as loss of coolant, anticipated transients without scram, and operational transients such 
as loss of feed water, etc. The multidimensional neutron kinetics model in RELAP5-3D 
is based on the NESTLE code 
9, which solves the two or four group neutron diffusion 
equations in either Cartesian or hexagonal geometry. A diffusion solution of the flux in 
four energy groups may however not be sufficient to get an accurate representation of 
the power distribution for the MHTGR 
10. In addition, linear cross section feedbacks on 
thermal hydraulic state parameters (such as fuel and moderator temperatures) as 
supported by NESTLE/RELAP5-3D are not detailed enough to capture the complex 
MTHGR cross section behaviour. 
To be able to simulate MHTGR transients with the required neutronic fidelity, the 
neutronic code package PHISICS has been coupled to RELAP5-3D. PHISICS (Parallel 
and Highly Innovative Simulation for INL Code System) is a neutronics code system in 
development 6 at INL. The different modules for PHISICS are a nodal and semi-
===PAGE 9===
8 
 
structured spherical harmonics based transport core solver (INSTANT 11) for steady 
state and time dependent problems, a depletion module (MRTAU 12) and a cross 
section mixer-interpolator (MIXER 13) module. A detailed discussion of PHISICS’ theory 
and structure are provided for interested readers14. This flexible package provides the 
functionality to cope with the challenges of coupled neutronic/thermal hydraulic MHTGR 
core simulations.  
Each different module of PHISICS contains a kernel (module) that solves a basic 
problem. A local driver is assigned to each kernel which is able to run it in standalone 
mode. Communication between the kernels is managed by the use of global data types 
that hold global information (cross section data, mesh, fluxes, etc.) that are needed by 
more than one kernel to perform complex calculations involving different kernels. Global 
drivers solving a complex problem calling different kernels can be developed easily with 
this flexible software structure. The PHISICS code is still in development to extend its 
capabilities (see 
14 for more detail).  
The transport core solver INSTANT (Intelligent Nodal and Semi-structured 
Treatment for Advanced Neutron Transport) is the key kernel of the PHISICS 
framework. INSTANT is parallelized and is designed to take full advantage of medium to 
large clusters (10 to 1000 processors). It is based on the second order formulation of 
the transport equation discretized in angle by spherical harmonics while in space it uses 
orthonormal polynomials of an arbitrary order. In addition to steady-state solutions, 
INSTANT is able to solve time dependent problems. For that, a scheme based on a 
second order backward Euler scheme with explicit delayed neutron treatment has been 
implemented as a new module for the PHISICS suite 
5. 
===PAGE 10===
9 
 
MRTAU (Multi-Reactor Transmutation Analysis Utility) is a generic depletion code 
developed at INL 12. In addition to core depletion, the code can be utilised for stand-
alone decay heat calculations. It tracks the time evolution of the isotopic concentration 
of a given material accounting for nuclear reactions occurring in the presence of neutron 
flux, and also due to natural decay (Bateman equation). The code uses a Taylor series 
expansion based algorithm at arbitrary order and the Chebyshev Rational 
Approximation Method (CRAM) for computation of the exponential matrix.  
The MIXER module does all the cross section handling for the different kernels. 
MIXER can handle macroscopic, microscopic and “mixed” cross sections. A 
macroscopic cross section library contains macroscopic cross sections for each type of 
material used in the calculation (fuel, reflector, etc.) tabulated for the state parameters 
(temperature, burn- up, control rod position, etc.). MIXER interpolates these cross 
sections at the requested state parameters, and no limits in tabulation dimensions or 
neutron energy groups exist. A microscopic or “mixed” cross section library contains the 
tabulated cross sections for each isotope considered in the calculation. MIXER reads a 
description containing a list of isotopes and corresponding de nsities for each material , 
and interpolate the microscopic cross sections at the requested state parameters . 
Macroscopic cross sections for each material are generated with the corresponding 
number densities.  
With this capability, “mixed” macroscopic and microscopic cross sections are also 
possible. For example, it is possible to provide macroscopic absorption cross sections 
without xenon for a material and the microscopic xenon absorption cross section 
together with a xenon density. MIXER will calculate the xenon absorption contribution 
and add it to the macroscopic cross section. MIXER can read different cross section 
library formats. Among them is an original simple XML based format, but also AMPX , 
===PAGE 11===
10 
 
ISOTXS and ECCO library formats that allow cross section libraries to be prepared with 
SCALE, ERANOS or MC2. More library types are planned to be supported in the future. 
 
III.B. The coupling of PHISICS to RELAP5-3D 
In order to increase the neutronic capabilities of RELAP5-3D, a direct coupling with 
the PHISICS code has been performed, i.e., PHISICS is integrated with RELAP5-3D as 
a set of subroutines14. This gives the user access to the full capability of PHISICS from 
within RELAP5-3D. The PHISICS part of coupled calculations can be parallelized on 
multiple processors, where the calculation is always driven by RELAP5-3D. The 
RELAP5-3D input reader decides if parts of PHISICS are needed and calls the 
PHISICS/RELAP5-3D driver accordingly. The driver collects the available input data 
from RELAP5-3D (geometry, calculation options) and adds, if needed, data from special 
PHISICS input files (cross sections, transport solver and depletion parameters, etc.) to 
complete the required information for the specified calculation. Once all the inputs are 
collected, the PHISICS/RELAP5-3D driver calls the required modules and feeds back 
the results (power distribution) to RELAP5-3D for the next iteration. Fig. 4 shows the 
general input data flows between the two packages. 
RELAP5-3D is interfaced with the PHISICS code through a direct software “pipeline” 
(interface) that manages the exchange of information between the two “physics” 
elements (TH feedbacks from RELAP5-3D to PHISICS, fission and decay heat power 
distributions from PHISICS to RELAP5-3D, etc.).  
This “direct” coupling allows the user to run existing RELAP5-3D input decks with 
INSTANT as the core solver (this option is made accessible from within RELAP5-3D 
===PAGE 12===
11 
 
through a new keyword). The coupling is compatible with the existing RELAP5-3D cross 
section and feedback options. Existing cross section inputs and mappings from kinetic 
nodes to thermal hydraulic zones can be used with the INSTANT option. The RELAP5-
3D control rod model can also be used. In addition, the coupling supports a new cross 
section option which relaxes the RELAP5-3D limitation of four energy groups (only if 
INSTANT is used). The number of neutron energy groups is only limited by the available 
computer memory. Multi-group cross sections for this new option support an unlimited 
number of tabulation dimensions (fuel temperature, moderator temperature, control rod 
position, xenon density, etc.) and an unlimited number of tabulation points per 
dimension. This new cross section option is compatible with existing mappings from 
kinetic nodes to thermal hydraulic zones in RELAP5-3D.  
The depletion code MRTAU is also coupled to RELAP5-3D as part of the PHISICS 
suite. It can be used to perform core depletion calculations with coupled thermal 
hydraulic feedbacks. In addition, MRTAU is able to burn the core to a desired depletion 
level, or to perform cooling (decay), before a transient is initiated. MRTAU can also be 
used for decay heat calculation and poison tracking during transients. The possible 
calculation paths resulting by combining INSTANT, MRTAU and RELAP5-3D are 
manifold 
14. In this paper, a summary of the calculation path used for the OECD/NEA 
MHTGR-350 MW benchmark are briefly presented. The calculation path can be split 
into two main classes, namely the “depletion time evolution” and the “time dependent” 
calculation path.  
The “depletion time evolution” scheme is used to compute core depletion with an 
updated temperature field in the core. Fig. 5a shows the calculation path for this 
scheme. The first loop (on the left in the figure) is iterated between MIXER, INSTANT 
and RELAP5-3D until temperature convergence is reached taking into account the 
===PAGE 13===
12 
 
neutronic feedback. The calculation then goes into the depletion loop (shown on the 
right in the figure). Here, the thermal hydraulic state parameters are kept constant, but 
the isotopic densities from the depletion calculation are updated. The scheme continues 
to deplete the core with user-defined time steps until an update of the temperature field 
is requested by the user (in Fig. 5a, t=T
n). This “depletion time evolution” scheme is 
used in the MHTGR benchmark analysis to find the steady state temperature 
distribution in the core with xenon at its equilibrium value (see Phase I Exercise 3, 
Section V).  
The “time dependent” calculation scheme with MRTAU is used to track isotopic 
densities during a coupled thermal hydraulic transient (i.e. RELAP-3D in transient 
mode). Fig. 5b shows the calculation scheme for the “time dependent” mode. In this 
mode, MRTAU depletes the core for one time step, as specified by RELAP5-3D. The 
updated sources and cross sections are then sent to INSTANT which computes the new 
core power and flux distribution for the current time step. During the next time step 
RELAP5-3D calculates the new temperature distribution. The MIXER module then 
finally updates the cross sections for the new thermal hydraulic state variables and 
sends it to MRTAU for the next depletion step. This scheme is not demonstrated in this 
paper, but will be utilized for the calculation of time-dependent xenon build-up and 
decay during the control rod movements defined for Phase II Exercise 4 
2. 
III.C. Ring and block Model Descriptions 
Two different PHISICS/RELAP5-3D models have been set up for the benchmark to 
investigate the improvements that can be obtained with a refined spatial representation. 
One models the core as 9 homogenized rings whereas the higher fidelity model is 
refined to one-sixth of a fuel block (hereafter referred to as the “ring” and “block” 
===PAGE 14===
13 
 
models). The details and assumptions for both these models are briefly presented here, 
in addition to the overviews presented previously 3,4,5. Since the two models only differ 
in the way they model the core and reflector regions, the reactor vessel nodalization for 
RELAP5-3D is the same for both models and is shown in Fig. 6. The time-dependent 
junction #255 adjusts the inflow helium mass flow rate to obtain a target outlet helium 
temperature of 687°C through a control variable that combines the outlet pressure (6.39 
MPa), inlet helium temperature (259°C) and the total thermal power (350 MW) boundary 
conditions. In addition to the core region, the model includes the lower plenum (#175), 
coolant riser (#115), upper plenum (#125), outlet plenum (#170), vessel gap and the 
vessel metal structures. The benchmark does not require the modelling of the 
secondary system.  
In both the ring and block models, PHISICS uses a hexagonal mesh for the 
neutronics solution corresponding to a fuel or reflector block. One third of the core is 
modelled to make use of the 120
o azimuthal symmetry (see Fig. 1). The core is divided 
into 14 axial levels, two each for the upper and lower reflector and 10 levels for the core 
region. The 26 energy group cross-section library is read as separate data structures, 
and updates are performed on these base cross-section sets during the steady state 
convergence process in the MIXER module. The cross section updates are performed 
for four state parameters: moderator and fuel temperature, xenon-135 concentration 
and the hydrogen concentration (for the water ingress transient).  
The four-dimensional cross-section tables are designed to cover the initial steady-
state conditions and the expected ranges of the four selected instantaneous feedback 
parameters in the transients to be simulated in the benchmark. The reference tabulated 
cross-section set was generated from the DRAGON 4 lattice code using the end of 
equilibrium core number densities with 26 energy groups 
2.

14 
 
III.C.1 Ring model 
The ring model follows the common system-code homogenization approach of 
modelling the inner reflector, fuelled core region, and outer reflector as rings in 
cylindrical coordinates, with three additional rings representing the core barrel (CB), 
reactor pressure vessel (RPV), and outer air boundary layer (Fig. 7). The coarse axial 
mesh is discretized in 2 upper reflector, 10 core and 2 lower reflector elements, as 
shown Fig. 2. The core consists of 9 parallel flow channels representing each a ring in 
the core, i.e., there are three channels representing the inner reflector, three channels 
representing the three fuel rings, two channels representing the replaceable outer 
reflector and one channel representing the permanent outer reflector. Each channel is 
coupled to its own heat structure representing the fuel or graphite blocks in the 
corresponding core ring. The radii of the rings are calculated to represent the correct 
graphite volumes in each of the structures, and it includes corrections for the 2 mm 
gaps between the reflector blocks and the 3 mm gap between the outer reflector and 
the metallic core barrel.  
In addition, a total of seven core bypass flow channels represent the flow through 
the gaps between the graphite blocks, according to the specifications of the benchmark. 
A number of conduction and radiation sets are included in the model to account for 
radial conduction and axial radiation between the graphite structures in the inner, outer, 
top and bottom reflectors. Radial radiation heat transfer is also modelled between the 
outer reflector surface and the core barrel, and from the outer surface of the reactor 
vessel to the boundary air layer, as shown in Fig. 8. Adiabatic boundary conditions are 
applied at the top and bottom model boundaries, and the outer radial air layer is defined 
to be at a constant temperature of 30°C. 
===PAGE 16===
15 
 
 
III.C.2 The unit cell 
In the MHTGR core design, neutronic feedbacks on the fuel and moderator 
(graphite) temperatures are quite different. Therefore, it is desirable to have an accurate 
prediction of the fuel and moderator temperatures independently. To model the fuel 
region of the core, a cylindrical “unit cell” has been defined, consisting of a fuel 
compact, its surrounding matrix graphite and a helium coolant channel (Fig. 9). In this 
approach, the fuel compact (TRISO fuel particles embedded in graphite) is 
homogenization to a single region. The unit cell is constructed as follows:  
• The radius of the coolant hole corresponds to the actual design radius. 
• The distance between the coolant hole and the homogenized fuel region, i.e. the 
thickness of the inner graphite region, corresponds to the actual design distance so 
that the heat transfer distance is conserved. 
• The homogenized fuel region conserves the actual design fuel volume, i.e. the 
thickness of this region is determined by the fuel volume in the unit cell. 
• The size of the outer graphite region (outer ring in Fig. 9) is such that the total 
graphite mass in the unit cell is represented. 
At this stage detailed results for a representative TRISO fuel particle within the fuel 
compact is not available, but preliminary investigations have been performed using an 
additional heat structure in RELAP5-3D to represent the layers within the TRISO 
kernels. Despite the fact that the fuel compact is still homogenised, the use of a “unit 
cell” model leads to better fuel and moderator temperatures prediction than a 
homogenized core ring. In the ring model three unit cells are therefore used (one per 
===PAGE 17===
16 
 
fuel ring) to distinguish between the average fuel and moderator temperature in the 
inner, center and outer fuel rings. 
III.C.3 The block model 
In the block model, a single hexagonal block is explicitly represented by dividing it 
into six triangular elements, as shown in Fig. 10. Each of these triangles is represented 
by the unit cell heat structure in RELAP5-3D as described above. Each unit cell heat 
structure is connected to two hydraulic channels; one in the center representing the 
core coolant helium flow and one on the outer surface representing the gap flow 
between two fuel blocks. Heat transfer between the triangular elements occurs in two 
ways: inside a block via conduction, and between blocks via radiation across the 2 mm 
gap between the block faces. These connections are defined as part of the conduction 
and radiation enclosure sets in the RELAP5-3D model, in addition to the existing 
enclosure sets already defined for the ring model (Fig. 8). This block model can 
therefore provide a much more detailed temperature distribution compared to the ring 
model.  
In the case of the first fuel ring, for example, blocks 8-13 in Fig. 3 can now be 
represented with 6*6=36 triangular elements, which provide 36 data points per axial 
layer compared to only one value for the ring model. 
In theory, one could imagine representing the whole core including the reflector 
regions (91*14=1274 blocks) using the triangular block representation. Unfortunately, 
RELAP5-3D has some restrictions that lead to a limit on the fidelity that can be 
obtained: 
===PAGE 18===
17 
 
• Neutronic feedback zones: A feedback zone defines how feedback parameters 
(temperatures, densities, etc.) calculated by RELAP5-3D are averaged before 
passed to the neutronic solver as well as how the power computed in a node by the 
neutronic solver is distributed among the RELAP5-3D heat structures. The modelling 
of Ex. 3 required the use of kinetic feedback zones to manage the exchange of 
power (calculated by INSTANT) and temperature (RELAP5-3D) data. There is 
however a limit on the number of these data types that the code can process. It was 
initially envisaged to couple the triangular feedback elements on a one-to-one basis, 
i.e., one triangular node in RELAP5-3D would provide temperature data to a 
triangular node in INSTANT, which would then pass back the triangular power 
distribution to RELAP5-3D for the next iteration. Since the modelling of the full 
MHTGR-350 core graphite structures in a triangular geometry requires 7,644 kinetic 
nodes, a full core triangular model was not an option due the limitations in the 
number of kinetic zones available in RELAP5-3D. (A total of 7,644*2=15,288 nodes 
are actually required if the both the fuel and moderator temperature are to be 
resolved separately). On the neutronics side of the coupling, INSTANT therefore 
uses a hexagonal mesh for the neutronics solution, i.e. corresponding to a single 
fuel or reflector block. 
 
• Heat conduction/radiation enclosures: A heat conduction/radiation enclosure in 
RELAP5-3D contains all surfaces that conduct or radiate heat to each other. Due to 
input limitations, an enclosure in RELAP5-3D can currently only hold 99 surfaces. To 
put all surfaces in the core into one enclosure is therefore not possible. Furthermore, 
there can be only 99 enclosures in total in the model and finally, a heat structure 
===PAGE 19===
18 
 
surface can only be part of one conduction or one radiation enclosure, but not both. 
This limitations lead to the following model choices: 
o It is not possible to represent the whole core with one-sixth blocks. It was 
therefore decided to focus the RELAP5-3D triangular sub-division on the 
important regions of the active core region (Rings 4-6) and the two reflector 
rings next to the core (Rings 3 and 7). The RELAP5-3D block model (Fig. 11) 
therefore consists of “smeared-out” representation in Rings 1 and 2 (Blocks 1-
3) and 8-10 (Blocks 44-91), and a detailed triangular resolution in Rings 3-7 
(Blocks 8-43) (the block numbering is indicated in Fig. 3) . 
o It’s not possible to connect one heat structure surface to a conduction and 
radiation enclosure at the same time. Therefore, it has been decided to use 
the left side (inner) of each unit cell to model the conduction inside a block 
and the right side (outer) to model the radiation between blocks. This 
approach leads to two important unit cell model assumptions. First, it 
assumed that the outer side temperature of the unit cell represents the 
temperature at the outer surface of a block. This temperature is used to 
radiate the heat between blocks. Second, the inner surface temperature of 
the unit cell has to be used to compute the conduction between two one-sixth 
elements of a block. To minimize the impact of these (obviously approximate) 
assumptions, the model view factors (radiation) and conduction lengths 
(conduction) have been corrected accordingly.

III.C.4 Model Size and Computational Performance 
As can be expected, the increase in model resolution and subsequent larger 
temperature and power datasets resulted in significant penalties in terms of model size 
and computational performance. A comparison of the two PHISICS/RELAP5-3D models 
is presented in Table II. The RELAP5-3D run time of a typical converged steady-state 
solution for the thermal fluids standalone Ex. 2 increased from 0.1 h (ring model) to 5 h 
(block model). For Ex. 3, the coupled PHISICS/RELAP5-3D solution required 11 h to 
converge for the ring model, and 20 h for the block model. The run time for Ex. 3 
includes the use of 28 parallel processors, compared to a single processor for Ex. 2. 
The main question is therefore if this investment in model development, simulation time 
and post-processing lead to significant gains in the block model results, compared to 
the much simpler and faster ring model.   
 
IV. BENCHMARK RESULTS: PHASE I EXERCISE 2 
RELAP5-3D in stand-alone mode has been used to solve the four sub-cases of 
Exercise 2, which are defined as thermal fluids standalone calculations with fixed power 
density profiles in the fuel region. The four sub-cases are defined in order of increasing 
complexity (and summarized in Table III). Ex. 2a assumes that all coolant flow is 
through the core region, and only fixed thermophysical properties are specified, as 
shown in Table IV. For Ex. 2b, the bypass flow modeling in seven fixed bypass flow 
channels, totalling up to 11% of the total inlet flow rate (Table V), are required. Instead 
of the fixed thermophysical properties provided for Ex. 2a and Ex. 2b, the material 
properties prescribed for Ex. 2c vary as complex functions of fluence and temperature 
2. 
The definition of Ex. 2d combines the variable material properties with the requirement 
===PAGE 21===
20 
 
to model the bypass flow distribution explicitly through the 2-mm gaps between the 
blocks.  
The primary parameters of interest for Ex. 2 are the gas and solid temperatures and 
global parameters such as the pressure drop over the core and the inflow mass flow 
rate. The benchmark does however require participants to also report secondary 
parameters such as the calculated thermal conductivities, mass flow rate or velocities in 
bypass channels and heat transfer factors, since this data can be utilized to trace the 
source of possible differences in the primary parameters. In this paper, only a subset of 
the available data will be presented to illustrate the main characteristics for each of the 
exercises. Since the variances between Ex. 2a and Ex.2b - Ex.2d is relatively small, 
most of the data discussed in this section is for Ex. 2a.  
IV.A Exercise 2a  
The Ex. 2a fuel temperature distributions shown in Fig. 12 (ring model) and Fig. 13 
(block model) for the bottom core axial layer illustrate the increased spatial resolution of 
the block model over the ring model. A more detailed distribution of temperatures was 
obtained on the triangular mesh compared to three values per axial layer for the ring 
model. The ring model predicted significantly lower temperatures compared to the block 
model, which show for example radial temperature variations up to 300°C between the 
three fuel rings on this axial level (Fig. 13). This is because the block model is not a 
spatial refinement of the ring model (in the CFD-sense), but an independent model 
developed with its own assumptions and approximations. The treatment of radiation 
enclosures, conduction between the triangular elements and conduction individual gas 
flow connections to each face of the triangles in the block model are significantly 
different from the much simpler representation in the ring model, where one unit cell 
===PAGE 22===
21 
 
represents an entire axial layer of blocks. For example, since the power density is 
specified per block, the spatial power variation between two blocks in the same ring can 
be distinguished in the block model, leading to individual predictions of the temperatures 
in these blocks. In contrast, the power density in the ring model are volume-averaged 
over all 6 blocks in the inner ring (for example), which directly leads to an averaging 
effect on the fuel temperatures as well. It can therefore not necessarily be deduced that 
this model provides the most accurate temperature predictions without comparison to 
experimental values.  
The two examples presented here show how the local temperature hot spots (e.g. in 
the inner fuel ring fresh fuel blocks) and variances between the blocks can be identified 
using a higher resolution model. This capability is lacking in the ring model, and the 
resultant under-prediction can be important in design and safety studies, where the 
margins to fuel temperature limits need to be quantified. A more detailed spatial 
quantification of the peak fuel temperatures during loss of cooling or control rod 
withdrawal transients could assist in calculating the volume of fuel exposed to high 
temperatures and power densities, which are typically used as boundary conditions by 
fuel performance codes for fission product release calculations. This in turn might allow 
a core designer or utility to license a design for higher power levels, if it can be shown 
that the margins are sufficient. The additional effort required for the development of a 
more detailed modelling approach can therefore be justified based on the intended use 
and possible gains that can be derived from these models.  
The graphite temperature distribution is shown for the whole core region in Fig. 14 
(ring model) and Fig. 15 (block model). The relatively high inner reflector temperatures 
can be seen in both figures, since Ex. 2a did not include any bypass flow modelling in 
the reflectors. The block model (Fig. 15) show six equal values in the regions where a 
===PAGE 23===
22 
 
ring representation is used, e.g. blocks 1 and 2, but the detailed information available 
form this model is again apparent in the triangular regions of the core.  
As an example of the data that can be obtained from the detailed block model, the 
average fuel temperature axial profiles for the six triangular elements of block 9 are 
presented in Fig. 16. The block 9 average value can then be calculated from this data, 
and compared with the inner fuel ring temperature result obtained from the ring model, 
as shown in Fig. 16. The lower temperatures of the ring model (up to 10% in certain 
regions) are again apparent in this axial temperature profile, caused by the lack of 
individual block power density resolution.   
IV.B Exercises 2b-2d  
The inclusion of the 11% bypass flow in the Ex. 2b RELAP5-3D model leads to 
significant decreases in the inner and outer graphite reflector temperatures, as shown in 
Fig. 17. The block model predicts up to 41% lower temperatures in the inner reflector 
region. The effect on the fuel region is in the opposite direction (i.e., Ex. 2b exhibits 
higher fuel temperatures), but this difference is comparatively small (less than 10%). 
This is because a decrease from 100% to 89% of the nominal helium flow rate through 
the core still represents a very effective convective heat removal mechanism. It should 
be noted that Ex. 2b represents an unphysical MHTGR-350 scenario, since 
approximately 6% of all energy is deposited outside the fuel region in the reflectors via 
fast neutron and gamma interactions 
15. This non-local heat deposition is not included in 
the benchmark specification, and will lead to much higher reflector temperatures, which 
will counter the effects of these reflector bypass flows to some degree. 
 
 
===PAGE 24===
23 
 
The only difference between Ex. 2b and Ex. 2c is the addition of the variable 
thermophysical properties. The RELAP5-3D MHTGR-350 benchmark model for Ex. 2c 
uses dependent thermophysical properties for graphite to account for the fluence, as 
prescribed in the benchmark specification. The proper modeling of the decrease in 
thermal conductivity with an increase in fast flux exposure is especially important for fuel 
and graphite temperatures during the loss of cooling transients in Phase II. Only minor 
differences are observed between these two Phase I cases, since the convective heat 
transport of the active helium cooling during normal operation dominates the effects of 
changes in the material conductivity and specific heat. The difference between Ex. 2b 
and Ex. 2c will become more significant during the loss of cooling transients that will be 
performed for Phase II. The results are therefore not shown here. 
 
Exercise 2d combines the effects of bypass flows and variable thermophysical 
properties. The difference between Ex. 2c and Ex. 2d is the specification that the real 
geometry of the MHTGR-350 must be used to determine the converged bypass flows in 
the 2 mm gaps that exist between all blocks, as well as the 3.5 mm gap between the 
outer reflector and the CB. This exercise is designed to capture the high resolution and 
fidelity that Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) is capable of, but since the intended 
design use of RELAP5-3D is typically thermal system analysis of a lower fidelity, the 
current RELAP5-3D model can only approximately the real geometry of these gaps with 
a network of 1D pipe elements. The RELAP5-3D block model predicts a total of 14.2% 
bypass flow outside the core region, i.e. relatively close to the 11% value used in Ex. 2b 
and Ex. 2c.

V. BENCHMARK RESULTS: PHASE I EXERCISE 3 
Exercise 3 combines the solutions of the neutronics and thermal fluid domains, and 
therefore requires the use of a coupled solution methodology. The Ex. 3a model is 
created by combining the RELAP5-3D thermal fluid model developed for Ex. 2c (11% 
bypass flow, variable thermophysical properties) with the PHISICS kinetics, while Ex. 2d 
is used as the basis for Ex. 3b. In the PHISICS/RELAP5-3D solver scheme, the xenon 
equilibrium is obtained by using MRTAU to “burn” the core in 4 time steps of 2.5 days 
each, i.e. for a total of 10 days. This process involves four recalculations of the flux with 
INSTANT after every 2.5 days, using the scheme illustrated in Fig. 5a. A comparison 
between the eigenvalues and Control Rod (CR) worths obtained for Ex. 3a and Ex. 3b is 
summarized in Table VI for the ring and block models. 
 
For Ex. 3a, the difference between the ring and block models are relatively small 
(less than 160 pcm), and the predicted CR worths are within 80 pcm for the two models. 
Since the neutronics model is the same for the ring and block cases, these variances 
are caused by the refined feedbacks provided by RELAP5-3D. The block model 
eigenvalues are less than the ring model values for Ex. 3b, and the two CR worths are 
almost identical. The only differences between the two cases (3a and 3b) are the 
changes in the fuel (Doppler) and moderator temperatures due to the slight variation in 
the bypass flow distribution, which act as the drivers for the small variances predicted 
for the two exercises. 
 
The power density distribution reaches a maximum axial value on level 8 (634 cm 
from the bottom of the core). The power density distribution on level 8 is shown for Ex. 
===PAGE 26===

3a in Fig. 18, where a peak value of 11 W/cm3 is calculated in block 13. (The power and 
flux data are calculated on the hexagonal mesh, as explained in section III.C).  
A fast and a thermal flux data example is shown in Fig. 19 and Fig. 20 for two of the 
26 groups. The Ex. 3a data shown here was taken at axial level 8 where the axial flux 
profiles reach a maximum value. The fast flux peak in the fuel regions of the core is 
clearly visible in Group 1 (Fig. 19), whereas the peak thermal flux occurs in Group 20 
and in the inner reflector as the fast neutrons are thermalized (Fig. 20). 
 
As a final example, the average fuel temperature relative difference (%) between Ex. 
2d and Ex. 3b is shown for the bottom (Fig. 21) and top (Fig. 22) axial layers of the core. 
In the hot bottom regions of the core the differences are less than -8% (i.e. the 
temperatures are higher for Ex. 2d than Ex. 3b), but these variations increase as the 
colder upper regions of the core are reached, where differences up to 23% are 
calculated for block 29 (Fig. 22). 
 
A significant difference therefore exists between the axial temperature profiles of Ex. 
2c and Ex. 3b (shown in Fig. 23 for block 9, as an example). This is caused by the 
feedback kinetics of the coupled case, as opposed to the constant power profile 
specified in Ex. 2d. The cross -section feedbacks on the xenon concentration, control 
rod position and the fuel and moderator temperatures all contribute to this change in the 
axial temperature distribution.

VI. CONCLUSIONS 
 
Selected results for Phase I Exercises 2 and 3 of the OECD/NEA MHTGR -350 
benchmark were presented for two models: a conventional homogenized “ring” model 
and a higher fidelity triangular -based “block” model. It was shown that the RELAP5 -3D 
code can be applied to obtain detailed fuel and reflector temperature profiles in a 
prismatic HTGR core. Although a significant penalty was paid in terms of the 
computational resources required for the block model, the resultant gain in spatial 
fidelity justifies the development of such detailed models. Local temperature hot spots 
and variances between the blocks can be identified with the use of such a refined 
model, which can be of significant importance in reactor safety studies, where the 
margins to fuel temperature limits (for example) need to be quantified.  
In the next phase of the benchmark, the two models will again be compared for two 
loss-of-cooling transients, two water ingress events and a power load follow transient. It 
is expected that the higher spatial fidelity of the block model will be especially apparent 
during the reactivity insertion caused by the water ingress into the MHTGR core.
